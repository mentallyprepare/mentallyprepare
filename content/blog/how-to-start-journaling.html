How to Start Journaling (When You've Tried Before and Stopped)

By Mentally Prepare · 7 October 2026

You bought the nice notebook. You wrote three pages on the first night, two on the second, and by the end of the week it was in a drawer. Sound familiar?

Most people who want to journal have already tried and stopped. That usually isn't a discipline problem. It's a design problem: the habit was set up to be too big.

Here's a way to start that's small enough to keep going.

Why most journaling attempts stop

  • The bar is too high. If every entry has to be a full page of insight, tired nights become skipped nights.
  • It feels like homework. Students already write all day. Another writing task at night can feel like more of the same.
  • Blank pages are intimidating. "Write about your feelings" is a hard instruction when you don't know what you feel.
  • One missed day feels like failure. Streak thinking turns a single skipped night into "I'm bad at this", and then you stop.
  • No one reads it. For some people that's the point. For others, writing into silence makes it hard to keep showing up.

The one-line method

Instead of aiming for pages, aim for one honest line a night. That's it.

  • Write it at roughly the same time each night, so it attaches to something you already do, like brushing your teeth.
  • It can be about anything: a moment, a feeling, something someone said.
  • If you want to keep writing after the first line, keep going. If not, you're done.
  • If you miss a night, the next night is just the next night. No catching up.

One line sounds too small to matter. But a line you actually write beats a page you don't. And on many nights, one line turns into five without you planning it.

10 journaling prompts for students

Use these when you don't know where to start. Pick one, answer it in a sentence, and stop whenever you like.

  1. What's one moment from today I'd like to remember?
  2. What took more energy today than I expected?
  3. Who did I feel most like myself around this week?
  4. What am I avoiding thinking about right now?
  5. What would I tell a friend who had the day I just had?
  6. What's something small that went okay today?
  7. What do I miss, and what do I not miss, about how things used to be?
  8. What's one thing I'm worried about, and what's one thing I can actually do about it?
  9. When did I feel most alone today, and when did I feel least alone?
  10. What do I need more of this week?

What to do on nights you have nothing to say

Some nights you'll open the page and feel empty. That's normal. Try one of these:

  • Write what you can see or hear right now. It sounds odd, but describing the room often leads somewhere.
  • Write one word for how today felt. "Flat." "Loud." "Fine, I think." That counts.
  • Write "I don't know what to write." Then keep going for one more sentence.
  • Skip the night. That's allowed too. The habit survives a skipped night; it doesn't survive guilt.

Private journaling vs. writing to someone

A private journal is completely yours. No one reads it, so you can be as messy, unfair or uncertain as you need to be. For many people, that freedom is what makes it work.

Other people find it easier to keep writing when someone is on the other end, like a letter, a shared notebook or a pen pal. Knowing someone will read your line tomorrow can make tonight's line easier to write. It also changes what you write: less of a diary, more of a conversation.

Neither is better. If you've tried private journaling and kept stopping, trying a shared version might be the change that makes it stick.

A few small things that help

  • Keep it where you'll see it. A notebook on your pillow or a notes app on your home screen.
  • Don't reread too soon. Give entries a few weeks before you look back. It's easier to be kind to past-you with some distance.
  • Don't edit yourself. Spelling, grammar and making sense are all optional.
  • Keep it safe. If privacy matters, use a notebook you can keep with you or an app with a lock.

If writing ever leaves you feeling worse rather than lighter, stop for the night and reach out to someone instead. Journaling is a tool, not an obligation.

When to reach out for more support

Journaling can be a helpful way to notice how you're doing. If what you're noticing is persistent low mood, anxiety that won't settle, or thoughts that frighten you, please talk to your college counsellor, a doctor or a qualified mental-health professional. Writing is not a substitute for that support.

In India, Tele-MANAS offers round-the-clock mental-health support through the toll-free number 14416. You can find the service details on the Government of India's National Mental Health Programme page.

Outside India, Find A Helpline can help you locate support in your country. Our safety page lists more helplines.

If you feel you may harm yourself or cannot stay safe, seek urgent help through local emergency services or the nearest emergency department. Tell someone you trust and ask them to stay with you while you get help.

Tonight, you don't need a new notebook or a new routine. Just one honest line.
