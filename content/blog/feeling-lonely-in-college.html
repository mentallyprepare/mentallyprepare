Feeling Lonely in College? 8 Small Ways to Find Connection

By Mentally Prepare · 6 October 2026

It's 11 pm. Your roommate is on a call with their family, laughing in a language you don't really follow. Your phone is open on Instagram, where people from school are posting group photos from their new college like they've known each other for years.

You ate dinner alone again. Not because anyone was mean to you. Just because by the time you got to the mess, everyone had already sat down with their people.

And you're thinking: is it just me?

If you're feeling lonely in college, it doesn't mean you've failed at making friends. You might be missing home, struggling to move beyond small talk, or wishing someone would notice when you're having a difficult day.

There isn't one trick that makes connection happen. But there are small ways to give it more room, without forcing yourself to become the loudest person in the room.

Why you might feel lonely in college

College can change several parts of your life at once: where you live, who you see, what your days look like, and who you turn to after a bad day. The NHS identifies moving to a new school or university as one possible contributor to loneliness.

That change can look different for each student:

  • Your familiar people aren't nearby. A call home matters, but it isn't the same as sitting beside someone who already knows you.
  • Groups seem to have formed before you arrived. From the outside, a few people sitting together can look like a friendship circle you can't enter.
  • Language and cultural differences make conversations harder. You may understand the lecture but feel left out when the conversation switches languages at dinner.
  • You had little time for friendships before college. After years of coaching, entrance exams, and deadlines, making space for a social life can feel unfamiliar.
  • Your actual days look different from the college life you expected. Between lectures, commuting, laundry, and assignments, there may be fewer effortless moments than you imagined.

You don't have to explain your loneliness perfectly for it to matter. And you don't have to be in first year to feel it.

Being alone and feeling lonely aren't the same

Being alone describes a situation. Feeling lonely describes a gap between the connection you want and the connection you feel.

You might enjoy a quiet walk or eating by yourself. You might also feel disconnected in a crowded classroom or a busy group chat.

Ask yourself: Do I want more people around me, or do I want someone I can be more honest with?

Your answer can help you choose a next step. A bigger social circle and a closer friendship are different needs.

8 small things to try when you feel lonely in college

These are suggestions to experiment with, not a checklist you need to complete. Start with whichever feels manageable.

1. Tell one person a little more than “I'm fine”

You don't have to share everything. Try telling a friend, sibling, or trusted adult: “College has been lonelier than I expected. Can we talk for a bit?”

The NHS recommends talking about your feelings and setting small, achievable targets. Your first step can be one honest conversation, rather than a plan to change your whole social life.

2. Let your friendships change

Your first friendships don't have to be your only friendships. You can appreciate someone you met at orientation and still look for people you connect with differently.

If the first group you joined doesn't feel comfortable, try another setting: a lab partner, a society meeting, an elective, or a volunteering activity. You aren't locked into the social circle you found in week one.

3. Give one activity a regular place in your week

Choose something you would like even if it didn't immediately lead to a friendship: badminton, theatre, a reading group, a campus project, or a walk with a classmate.

Make the first goal small: “I'll attend once and see how it feels.” You can ask the organiser whether it's okay to watch before joining in.

4. Make a second conversation easier

After a conversation you enjoyed, look for a natural way to continue it.

“Are you coming to the meeting next week?”

“Want to sit together in tomorrow's class?”

This gives both of you a specific next moment. You don't need to turn one conversation into a close friendship immediately.

5. Use a small, specific invitation

“Let's hang out sometime” can be hard to respond to. A smaller invitation gives someone something clear to say yes to:

  • “I'm getting chai after class. Want to come?”
  • “Want to compare notes on this assignment?”
  • “Are you heading to the mess? Can I walk with you?”

They might be busy or decline. That can feel disappointing, but one response doesn't tell you what everyone thinks of you.

6. Keep room for the people who already know you

You don't have to stop leaning on old friends to make new ones. A familiar voice can matter on a difficult evening.

Instead of waiting until you feel terrible, arrange a short call that works for both of you. You can build a life in college while keeping the relationships that helped you get there.

7. Let yourself eat alone without turning it into a verdict

Eating alone can feel uncomfortable when you want company. But one meal doesn't tell the story of your whole college experience.

If asking to join a table feels too much today, have your meal anyway. Tomorrow, you could ask a classmate before lunch: “What time are you going to the mess?”

You deserve to eat, whether you have company or not.

8. Notice how you feel after scrolling

If group photos leave you feeling worse, experiment with a shorter scroll or mute an account for a while. See whether that changes your evening.

The NHS cautions against comparing yourself with what people choose to share on social media. Someone else's post isn't a timetable you have to follow.

Feeling lonely in a hostel? Start with the next day

At night, “I have no friends in college” can feel like a conclusion about your entire future. Try bringing it back to one day.

What would make tomorrow a little less disconnected?

Maybe it's having breakfast outside your room. Maybe it's asking someone to walk to class with you. Maybe it's booking an appointment with the college counsellor.

Choose one step. If it doesn't go as hoped, you can adjust it. You don't need to prove anything by doing all eight suggestions at once.

Three journaling prompts for a lonely evening

If writing feels useful, use these prompts to describe your day. You don't have to find a positive ending or turn the feeling into a lesson.

  1. When did I feel most alone today, and what was happening around me?
  2. Who is one person I felt even slightly comfortable with this week?
  3. What's one small step I could try tomorrow, and what support would make it easier?

One honest line is enough. “I don't know what I need yet” is an answer too. (If you want to make it a habit, here's how to start journaling when you've tried before and stopped.) If writing makes you feel more overwhelmed, you can stop and reach out to someone instead.

When to reach out for more support

You don't have to wait until things become unbearable. If loneliness is affecting your sleep, studies, appetite, or ability to enjoy your day, consider speaking with a college counsellor or qualified mental-health professional.

You can start with: “I've been feeling disconnected, and it's getting hard to manage.” You don't need a diagnosis or a perfect explanation to ask for help.

In India, Tele-MANAS offers round-the-clock mental-health support through the toll-free number 14416. You can find the service details on the Government of India's National Mental Health Programme page.

Outside India, Find A Helpline can help you locate support in your country. Our safety page lists more helplines.

If you feel you may harm yourself or cannot stay safe, seek urgent help through local emergency services or the nearest emergency department. Tell someone you trust and ask them to stay with you while you get help.

You deserve connection and support, whatever year you're in. Tonight, that can begin with one message: “Can we talk?”
