How to Make Friends in College When You Feel Shy

By Mentally Prepare · 30 October 2026

You can want friends and still dread walking up to a group. Maybe everyone in class seems to have found their people already. Maybe you can talk about an assignment, but you do not know how to turn that conversation into anything more.

To make friends in college, start where you already see the same people. Say one thing before or after class, return to a group or activity you enjoy, and suggest a small next meeting. Friendship usually needs more than one conversation. You do not need to become outgoing overnight.

These ideas work whether you live in a hostel, commute to campus, or have been at college longer than a semester. Try one that feels manageable and adjust it to your own comfort and safety.

1. Look for a place you can return to

A single big event can be exhausting. A class, library table, society meeting, volunteer shift, or weekly sports session gives you a reason to see the same people again. Familiarity makes the second hello easier.

Student Minds suggests looking at the places you already spend time, then choosing a few other opportunities through your college or students' union. If you are nervous, the NHS notes that you can ask to watch a group or class first.

2. Start with what is happening around you

You do not need an impressive opening line. A question about the next lecture or a shared task is enough to begin:

  • “Have you started the reading for Thursday?”
  • “Is this your first time at the club too?”
  • “Do you know when the library closes today?”

Listen to the answer. If the person seems interested, ask one follow-up. If they are busy, let the conversation end kindly. A short exchange still makes the next hello less unfamiliar.

3. Make a second conversation possible

After talking with someone you liked, give yourselves a natural reason to meet again. Try, “Are you going to the same workshop next week?” or “Want to sit together in tomorrow's class?”

This is a small invitation, not a demand for instant closeness. Student Minds recommends thinking about how to build on an initial class conversation rather than leaving every meeting to chance.

4. Invite one person to something specific

“We should hang out sometime” is easy to say but hard to arrange. A clear, low-pressure plan gives the other person a simple choice:

  • “I am getting chai after class. Want to come?”
  • “Would you like to revise together for half an hour on Friday?”
  • “I am going to the society meeting. Want to walk over together?”

If they cannot make it, that does not tell you what they think of you. You can try once more at another time or ask someone else.

5. Keep the first meeting easy to leave

A coffee between classes or a short walk can feel less intimidating than an open-ended evening. Choose a public place and a time that works for you. You can say, “I have about twenty minutes before my next class.”

Being shy does not mean you have to accept a plan that makes you uncomfortable. A good friendship leaves room for your boundaries.

6. Let a friendship grow at its own speed

Some people become close quickly. Others need many ordinary conversations. You can talk about a shared interest, remember something the person told you, or ask how their week went. Notice whether they also make space for you.

If you feel lonely even while spending time with people, you may be looking for a more honest conversation, not a larger group. Our article on feeling lonely around other people explores that difference.

7. Ask for support when it stays hard

It can take time to find people you feel comfortable with. If loneliness is making it difficult to sleep, study, eat, or enjoy your days, speak with someone you trust or your college counselling service. Student Minds also recommends using university support when loneliness persists.

You can say, “I have been finding it hard to connect with anyone here. Could we talk about what might help?” You do not need to wait for a crisis to ask.

One small plan for this week

  1. Choose one class, club, or activity you will attend again.
  2. Say hello to one person there and ask a simple question.
  3. If you enjoy talking, suggest one specific next meeting.

Three steps are a plan, not a test of your worth. If the first person is not a fit, you can try again with someone else. You can also read our guide to feeling lonely in college for more ways to find connection.
