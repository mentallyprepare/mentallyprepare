# What does feeling left out mean and what can you do about it

You can look fine in class, keep up with a group chat and still feel invisible when everyone makes plans without you. Feeling left out means you sense that you are outside a connection you want to be part of, whether anyone intended to exclude you or not.

Give yourself time to settle before deciding what happened. Then look at the evidence and, if the relationship matters to you, talk to the person involved.

## What does feeling left out mean?

Feeling left out means feeling excluded or overlooked by a person or group you want to belong to. It can feel like being outside the circle or like getting the cold shoulder.

It can happen at any age, from the playground to an assisted-living bridge game. A missed dinner or an unanswered group chat can hurt because connection matters to you.

The experience often unfolds in three stages: you discover what happened, feel the hurt or anger, and eventually get enough distance to see the situation and your own part in it more clearly.

## Left out feeling vs being left out

Someone may deliberately exclude you, or you may feel excluded when nobody intended it. The difference matters when you decide what to do next.

An organiser may have assumed someone else told you, thought you weren't interested or made a spontaneous plan without thinking about who was missing. Social media makes that harder to judge because you see the best parts of other people's lives and can get the impression that everyone else is happy without you.

If you know the exclusion was deliberate, reflect gently on what happened, either on your own or with someone you trust. Put some energy towards friends who make you feel good about yourself, too.

## Why does feeling left out hurt so much?

Belonging has deep roots. For our ancestors, a group offered access to resources and protection from danger. The instinct to stay connected is still with us.

Sometimes the missed event isn't the worst part. It can hurt to see friends go along without you, or stand by while someone else excludes you. You may have expected them to speak up and discover that they were willing to carry on with the plan.

That hurt can turn into a loop of “Why am I the friend who gets left out?” You might feel lonely, criticise yourself or get angry. Anger can also be easier to feel than the hurt underneath it.

## What does feeling left out feel like?

You might feel rejected and wonder what you did wrong, even if the exclusion was accidental. That can bring anxiety and make you less confident around the same people.

You might also feel forgotten. A move, a new job or having a child can change how much time friends have for one another. When shared experiences become less frequent, the distance can be hard to miss.

If a friendship fades, you may feel that the time you put into it was wasted, or feel disheartened that it meant less to the other person than it did to you. The good memories can still matter, and the relationship may have taught you something.

Sadness, anger and confusion can arrive together. Give yourself room to feel them rather than trying to force them away.

## Why do friends make you feel left out?

Friends sometimes leave someone out because they think that person has broken an unwritten rule, isn't contributing to the group or has a trait they judge negatively. A group can also grow closer around a shared dislike of someone. People who offend others or come across as smug may find themselves excluded more often.

In seven University of Basel studies involving nearly 2,400 participants, strategic motives for ostracism were stronger than stigma. Exclusion reached 87.5 percent in the most extreme experimental condition.

## What it means to feel like a third wheel

You can feel like a third wheel when you're included in a plan but the closeness runs between the other two people. You might be sitting with them while they make plans in front of you that don't include you.

For more on that dynamic and what to do, see [Third Wheel: What It Really Means and What to Do When You Feel Like One](https://mymentallyprepare.com/third-wheeling-meaning/).

## Feeling left out at university

When your closest friend moves to a different class, breaks and lunchtime can suddenly feel very long. It can seem as though everyone else already has their people, but there's room for more friends.

Look around your class for someone you'd actually like to spend time with. Say hello, share something or ask, “Do you want to sit here?” at lunch. Small invitations give you a way to get to know someone without having to walk into an established group all at once.

If you're shy, ask a teacher for help connecting with people. Kindness and offers of help can also make it easier for a new friendship to take shape.

## What is emotional loneliness?

Emotional loneliness is the feeling that you lack the close connection you need. Repeated moments of feeling left out can build into it, especially when you have people around you but don't feel known by them.

## Why do I always feel out of place everywhere?

You may feel out of place because fitting into a group often involves adjusting to its interests, values and way of talking. If that feels like too much of a trade, you can become conscious of being out of sync, grow anxious and start avoiding social situations.

You might notice that you speak up comfortably at work or on a sports team, then freeze at a purely social gathering where you feel judged.

Consider Clara, who stopped trying to become someone else around her group. She admitted she'd never held a golf club and asked about ingredients she didn't know. She was accepted as part of the group while skipping its golf tournaments and gourmet dinners.

You don't have to share every interest to stay connected. Try asking about what you don't know and joining the parts you actually enjoy.

## Is loneliness a symptom of depression?

Being upset after one experience of exclusion is a normal response. On its own, it isn't a sign of depression.

Ongoing loneliness and repeated rejection can take a toll on your mental health. If those feelings are staying with you, support is worth arranging.

## What to do when you feel left out

Start by giving yourself a little space. You can decide what the situation needs once the first rush of hurt has eased.

### How do you calm down when you feel left out?

Hold off on sending the angry message or confronting someone while you're still caught in the first reaction. Assumptions made in that moment can lead to conflict you didn't need.

Take a walk and pay attention to the ground under your feet. Try slow breathing, write down what you're feeling, or name the people and places that help you feel safe.

### How do you tell if you were actually excluded?

Look for evidence in both directions. If you think your friends no longer care, ask yourself what supports that conclusion and what suggests they do care. If there's more evidence that they care, another explanation is likely.

Think about what you know of the plan, too. A photo shows a small part of an evening. Ask yourself whether you would actually have gone and whether something else is making the fear of missing out stronger right now.

Notice the signals you've been sending. If you've turned down several invitations, your friends may assume you're busy or uninterested. You might also have missed plans being discussed while you were absorbed in something else.

### How do you tell a friend you feel left out?

With a close friend, it's usually worth bringing up. With a casual coworker, you may decide the conversation isn't worth the day-to-day awkwardness.

Be specific about what happened and how you felt. You could say:

> “I heard you planned a group dinner. I felt bad that I wasn't invited. Did I do anything to upset you? Should we talk about this?”

Listen to the answer, even if it's uncomfortable. A direct conversation can help you work through a rupture. Repeated excuses or avoidance are worth noticing.

If you've been unavailable for a while, you can also make your interest clear: “I know I've been busy lately, but I've got plenty of free time now! Do you have time to catch up next week?”

### How do you stop feeling left out over time?

Give yourself something kind to do on a night you've missed a plan. Put your phone away, read a book, take a bath or choose another way to feel cared for.

Start with the relationships that already feel good. Reconnecting with someone you know can be quicker than building a friendship from scratch because you already have shared history. If you need more opportunities to meet people, try a book club, a group workout or an industry event and notice which connections feel good.

It also helps to reflect honestly, on your own or with someone whose opinion you value. Do you tend to take up a lot of space? Have you overlooked a conflict or asked more of the group than people can give?

One writer called a host weeks after being excluded to apologise for anything she'd done to harm his marriage. He denied there was a problem and said space had been limited. She felt free of her victim status when the call ended.

## When should you get help for feeling left out?

If you're often on the fringe of social interactions, or loneliness and rejection keep weighing on you, a therapist can help you examine your relationships and build ways to cope. Therapy can also help with persistent self-criticism and thoughts that you're unworthy of friendship.

If you're in acute distress, Crisis Text Line offers free, confidential support from a live volunteer crisis counselor, 24/7. Text HOME or HOLA to 741741.
